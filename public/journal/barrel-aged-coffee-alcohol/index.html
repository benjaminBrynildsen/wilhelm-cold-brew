    Does barrel-aged coffee contain alcohol?

    We get asked this more than any other question. The answer for our coffee is no, and not as a matter of opinion. It's settled by a temperature.

    The short answer

    Our beans go into the bourbon barrel green — raw, unroasted. They come out and get roasted, which takes them past 200 °C. Ethanol boils at 78.37 °C. Whatever alcohol the wood gave up is gone during the roast, before the coffee is ever brewed. What survives is the oak.

    How ours is actually made

    Worth laying out, because the answer depends entirely on it.

    Green coffee goes into an emptied bourbon barrel and sits. Weeks, sometimes months. Green beans are dense, dry and porous, and they spend that whole time taking on what the wood has to give.

    How long is genuinely variable, and anyone who tells you there's one right number is selling something. It moves with the coffee — variety, density, moisture. It moves with the barrel — how recently it was dumped, how much spirit the staves still hold, how heavily it was charred. And it moves with what you're after. Every producer runs it differently. The same producer runs it differently batch to batch.

    Then the beans come out and get roasted.

    From there it's our cold brew process, which is slow and fussy in ways we'll write about separately. The finished brew is bottled, then pasteurized — heated in the sealed bottle to make it safe and shelf-stable, so what you open months from now tastes like what we made.

    One thing to be clear about, because other producers do it differently: our liquid never touches the barrel. The oak is on the green bean. By the time there's cold brew, the barrel's work is long finished.

    Why there's anything in the barrel to begin with

    American bourbon has to be aged in new charred oak.[3] One barrel, one run of bourbon, and then a distillery owns an expensive piece of cooperage it can never legally use for bourbon again. That rule is the reason used barrels exist at all — for scotch producers, for brewers, for us.

    A dumped barrel isn't empty. Oak is porous, and years of maturation soak a real volume of spirit into the staves. Distillers and coopers routinely put it at several gallons still held in the wood of a 53-gallon barrel after draining. The barrel we receive is still wet.

    So there is ethanol present when our beans go in. The question is what becomes of it.

    What the barrel actually gives you

    Mostly not alcohol.

    The flavors people associate with barrel aging — vanilla, a coconut-ish woodiness, faint smoke, caramel weight — come from the oak and from what heat did to the oak when it was toasted and charred. Spirits researchers have characterized these compounds thoroughly.[1][2]

    None of those is alcohol. In a whiskey barrel, ethanol's main job is being a solvent — it's good at pulling these compounds out of wood. But the compounds are the flavor. Alcohol is the delivery van, not the cargo.

    The barrel isn't lending you bourbon. It's lending you oak, and the ghost of what the oak went through.

    Why the roast settles it

    Ethanol boils at 78.37 °C.[5] A coffee roast takes beans past 200 °C and holds them there for minutes.[6]

    Nothing meaningful survives that. The ethanol is driven off early, well before first crack, and vented away.

    What makes this useful rather than merely lucky is that the flavor compounds don't behave the same way. Vanillin, the oak lactones, the furanic aldehydes — all considerably less volatile and more thermally durable than ethanol. They stay. The roast is selective: it takes the alcohol and leaves the oak.

    That asymmetry is the only reason barrel-aged coffee can exist as a non-alcoholic product at all.

    What pasteurizing does, and doesn't

    Our bottles are pasteurized after filling. That's a food-safety step — heat holds off spoilage organisms so the coffee keeps without preservatives.

    It isn't an alcohol step, and we'd rather not let anyone assume it is. Pasteurizing happens in a sealed bottle. Nothing evaporates out of a closed container; volatiles have nowhere to go. The roast is what removes ethanol, because the roast is an open system with a vent. Pasteurizing keeps the coffee safe and stable, and that's the whole of its job.

    The method where the question gets harder

    There's a second thing people mean by "barrel-aged coffee," and it behaves differently.

    Instead of aging green beans, some producers put finished liquid into the barrel — brewed coffee resting in oak the way a spirit would. No roast follows. Liquid sits against wet wood, and ethanol in the staves can migrate into it.

    How much depends on how recently the barrel was dumped, how wet it remains, the ratio of wood surface to liquid, the temperature, and the length of contact. The amounts are usually small; a barrel refilled with a water-based liquid is a very different place than one holding 125-proof spirit, and the pressure pushing ethanol into the coffee is weak. Most producers who test come in far under the legal line.

    But "usually" and "most" are doing real work in those sentences. A producer using liquid aging should test their product rather than reason their way to a comfortable answer. We don't use that method today, so the question doesn't arise for what's in your bottle.

    Where the 0.5% line comes from

    In the US, 0.5% alcohol by volume is the conventional threshold below which a drink counts as non-alcoholic.[4] Two things worth knowing about it.

    It isn't zero. And it isn't a strange amount of alcohol to find in food — ordinary fermentation puts trace ethanol into things nobody thinks about. Ripe fruit. Some breads. Kombucha. A very ripe banana is not a cocktail.

    For coffee aged green and then roasted, this threshold is mostly beside the point. The roast isn't a process that gets you under a limit. It's one that removes the thing being measured.

    What we can't tell you yet

    The chemistry above comes from decades of serious work on spirits maturation: how oak gives up its compounds, what toasting and charring produce, how extraction proceeds over time. That literature is deep and it's reliable.

    The literature on barrel-aged coffee is not. It's thin, recent, and mostly hasn't been through peer review. A lot of what circulates as fact in specialty coffee is borrowed from wine and spirits research — sometimes a fair borrow, sometimes not. When someone tells you precisely how much vanillin migrates into a green bean over sixty days, ask where the number came from.

    We'd rather mark the line between established chemistry, reasonable inference, and our own observation from running batches. We'll keep marking it, including when the honest answer is less flattering than the confident one.
